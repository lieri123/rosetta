# Rosetta

Photograph a page of handwriting, get text you can post — not a picture of your
handwriting, and not a wall of plausible-looking guesses either. Words the
system is unsure about are underlined, and it tells you *why* it is unsure.

The idea holding it together: recognition APIs return more than text. They
return a confidence for every token, and sometimes alternative readings.
Almost nobody uses either. If you do, you can treat the API's output as a noisy
observation of what was actually on the page and recover the likelier truth:

```
P(true | observed)  ∝  P(observed | true) · P(true)
```

The first term is an error model fitted from your own corrections. The second
is a language prior built from your own writing. The same machinery that picks
the better reading also decides what to underline, which is why the marks mean
something specific rather than being a second heuristic bolted alongside.

---

## The underlines

Two different kinds of doubt, so two different marks.

| Mark | Means | Comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Red wavy** | The recogniser could barely read the ink. Probably garbage. | Recognition confidence below threshold |
| **Amber wavy** | It read this confidently, but the word does not belong here — or the rescorer nearly chose something else. | Language model surprisal, or a narrow margin between the top two candidates |
| *nothing* | Confident and unsurprising. | — |

The amber tier is the one that earns its keep. A plausible-looking wrong word
comes back from the API with *high* confidence, because the recogniser found a
clean reading — just not the one on the page. A threshold on confidence alone
cannot see those at all. Only a model of context can.

Hovering a mark shows what was read, how confident the recogniser was, why it
was flagged, and — when there is one — a suggested reading you can accept with
one click. Accepting it is also a correction, which is to say training data.

---

## The loop

This is the part worth understanding, because everything else serves it.

1. You fix a word in the editor. That gives an aligned pair, `(predicted,
   corrected)`.
2. Levenshtein alignment pulls character-level evidence out of the pair.
   Adjacent errors are kept together, so `rn → m` is recorded as one confusion
   rather than as an unrelated insertion and substitution.
3. Those accumulate into a confusion matrix that is *yours*. Not "handwriting
   is hard" — your `a` reads as `o` 14% of the time, your `5` and your `S` are
   indistinguishable, your `rn` collapses into `m`.
4. The corrected text also joins your lexicon and your language model. One
   correction teaches three things at once.
5. Next page: low-confidence tokens get candidate readings generated by
   applying those confusions *backwards*, each scored against the channel and
   the prior, and substituted when the margin is decisive.

`make demo` runs exactly this on a synthetic page, end to end, with no API key:

```
6. What came back
    accumulating those alignments bullds a personal confusion matrlx
    thc matrlx shows my own failurc modes rafher than an avcrage writer's
    ...
    9 wrong words. Underlined: 3 red, 7 amber, 48 left clean.

7. Correcting it, the way you would in the editor
    4 corrected line(s) sent, 4 learned
    the confusion matrix now holds 28 character pairs:
      'e' read as 'c': 5 times (20% of the time)
      'i' read as 'l': 3 times (18% of the time)

8. The same page again
    identical image, identical recogniser output — only the post-processor changed
    accumulating those alignments builds a personal confusion matrix
    the matrix shows my own failure modes rather than an average writer's
    ...
    9 wrong words became 0, with 11 rewritten by the rescorer
```

Note what happened in step 8: words that were never corrected — `ohly`,
`dccisive` — were fixed too, because the *character-level* confusions
generalise beyond the specific words they were learned from.

## Does it actually beat the raw API?

There is an evaluation harness, because "it feels better" is not a claim.
`make eval` measures the decoder against the recogniser's own output on
**held-out** lines:

```
metric                     baseline       cold    adapted  vs baseline
----------------------------------------------------------------------
character error rate         0.0651     0.0639     0.0575     -0.0077 better
word error rate              0.2929     0.2838     0.2498     -0.0431 better

substitutions 6 (helped 6, hurt 0)
flag precision 0.800  recall 0.686  f1 0.738
```

Three columns because there are three distinct claims and conflating them would
overstate the result: the API alone, the decoder before it has seen any of your
corrections, and the decoder after 24 of them. About 12% relative CER
reduction, 15% on words.

**These numbers come from a synthetic error model**, not from a recogniser —
clean text corrupted through known shape-collision confusions, with a
deliberate minority of errors arriving at high confidence. Anything measured
that way is labelled synthetic wherever it is reported. Point the harness at
`--pairs` with real recognised/corrected pairs once you have them, and the same
table comes out with numbers you can trust further.

Two defaults were set *by* the harness rather than by instinct, which is most of
why it exists:

- **Substitution margin** was 3.0 nats on gut feel. Sweeping it showed
  substitutions helping 14 times and hurting zero across three seeds, with CER
  rising monotonically above 1.0 — the instinctive setting was leaving two
  thirds of the good corrections unmade. Now 1.0.
- **Amber has three triggers.** A word is marked amber when the rescorer
  substituted it (the winner beat the reading by at least substitute_nats,
  1.0), when the top two candidates were within close_call_nats (1.5) of each
  other, or when recognition confidence was middling (below 0.86) and the
  language model gave the word weak support (above unsettled_bits, 9 bits of
  surprisal). Anything below 0.55 confidence is red first.

-  **The "improbable in context" amber is off by default.** It used to fire above 15 bits of surprisal, but every word the language model has never seen gets the same floor surprisal, and that floor rises with the size of the notes: 13.4 bits at ~650 words, 15.7 at ~3,600. So a fixed threshold never fired on small notes and fired on *every* new word on large ones. It can still be switched on with Thresholds(surprisal_bits=...).

## How it is built

```
  photo ──► C preprocessing ──► Go service ──► recognition API
                                    │               │
                                    │               ▼
                                    │        Python rescorer
                                    │               │
                                    ▼               ▼
                                 SQLite ◄─── text + confidence spans
                                    │
                                    ▼
                          TypeScript + CodeMirror
```

**C — preprocessing.** Perspective correction, deskewing and adaptive
binarisation, written against raw pixel buffers. Compiled to a static binary
the Go service shells out to; the JSON it prints is the interface between them.
Shelling out rather than cgo, because the C is a batch transform over a file
with a report — a shape a subprocess models exactly — and it keeps the Go
cross-compiling, keeps the C independently testable, and keeps a crash in the
image code from taking the server down. `stb_image` is vendored for decode and
encode: the interesting part is the transforms, not writing a PNG parser.

**Go — service layer.** HTTP API, PDF page splitting, a worker pool, recognition
calls, SSE for progress. This is where Go's concurrency does real work rather
than being a checkbox: pages are independent and spend nearly all their time
waiting on a subprocess and then a remote API, so a fifty-page notebook is a few
seconds instead of a coffee break. Jobs live in the database rather than a
channel, so a crash mid-page is recoverable, and claiming is an
`UPDATE ... WHERE state = 'queued'` — on a paid API, a job handed out twice is a
bill as well as a bug.

**Python — the rescorer.** The one place where consolidating on a single
language would be a mistake. Levenshtein alignment, confusion-matrix fitting,
n-gram modelling and the evaluation harness are all dramatically faster to write
here, and fighting Go for numerical work would cost days and buy nothing. It
runs as a small HTTP service the Go layer calls. Standard library only, no
dependencies; matplotlib is optional and only draws curves the harness has
already computed.

**SQLite — storage.** Documents, pages, tokens, corrections, the confusion
matrix, the confidence spans. One file, zero operations, comfortable with years
of one person's notes. Postgres would be pure ceremony at this scale.

**TypeScript + CodeMirror 6 — the editor.** No framework. The app is one editor
view with decorations on it; React would add a build to maintain and nothing
else.

**Deployment.** One VPS, `docker compose up`, or systemd units. Not Kubernetes.

### Three design decisions worth defending

**Character offsets are computed on the server.** The service builds the page
text and records where each token landed in it, in the same pass. The browser
never re-tokenises, so it cannot disagree about where a word starts, and an
underline cannot drift onto the wrong word. A test asserts that slicing the text
by each token's offsets returns that token.

**An untrained system does nothing.** The confusion model carries a pseudo-count
on its diagonal, so before it has seen a correction it behaves like plain edit
distance and gets out of the way. Without that, a fresh install has a uniform
channel and the language prior cheerfully rewrites correct text into commoner
words. A test pins it: a cold rescorer changes nothing.

**Unknown words survive.** A character n-gram model runs alongside the word
model, so a word the system has never seen keeps a finite probability. Without
it, every name, every piece of jargon and every bit of notation you invented
last week would be "corrected" into the nearest familiar word.

### Every step degrades rather than fails

No preprocessor built? Pages are recognised unprocessed — worse, but not
nothing. Rescorer unreachable? Text still comes back, tiered on confidence
alone — and in that state **no amber tier is produced at all**, because amber
means "a language model finds this improbable" and there is no language model
to say so. Putting the same underline on a different claim would be a lie to the
reader.

## Strikethrough

Crossed-out words should not end up in the text. The contained version of the
hard-layout problem, and it demos well:

1. The C finds near-horizontal strokes with a restricted Hough transform — the
   page has already been deskewed, so only a narrow band of angles is worth
   searching, which turns an expensive general transform into a cheap one.
2. It reports geometry and stops there. Whether a stroke is a strikethrough
   depends on word boxes, which do not exist until after recognition.
3. The service asks the question that matters: does the stroke pass through the
   *middle* of a word's box, overlapping most of its width? Through the middle
   is a deletion. Underneath is emphasis, and the word stays.

Both guards exist because the failure modes are wildly asymmetric. Leaving a
crossed-out word in is a nuisance; deleting a word the writer meant to keep is
data loss they may not notice.

## Running it

```bash
make            # build the C, the Go and the web assets
make test       # all three suites
make demo       # the whole pipeline on a synthetic page, no API key needed
```

Then, in two terminals:

```bash
make run-rescorer
make run-service     # http://127.0.0.1:8800
```

Or `cd deploy && docker compose up --build`.

### Seeding it

A fresh install knows nothing about your vocabulary. Point it at writing you
already have:

```bash
cd rescorer
python3 -m rosetta_rescorer --db ../data/rosetta.db ingest ~/notes/*.md
```

### Choosing a recogniser

The default provider is **mock** — it produces plausible words, boxes,
confidences and confidently-wrong readings, deterministically, so the whole
pipeline is demonstrable and testable without an API key. Recognition is the
only step that costs money per page and sends your ink to someone else's
computer, so it is opt-in:

```bash
export ROSETTA_PROVIDER=google GOOGLE_VISION_API_KEY=...
```

| | What it returns | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Google Cloud Vision** (`google`) | Full block → paragraph → word → symbol hierarchy, confidence at every level, bounding quadrilaterals | `DOCUMENT_TEXT_DETECTION` mode. The per-symbol confidences are the signal this project is built on; plain `TEXT_DETECTION` does not return them |
| **Azure Image Analysis 4.0** (`azure`) | Lines and words with confidence and bounding polygons | No symbol level. Synchronous, so no operation polling |
| **Mock** (`mock`) | Deterministic pseudo-page with realistic error families | Default. No network, no key, no bill |

Both providers sit behind one interface, so swapping is a config change and
comparing them on the same pages is a matter of running twice. **I have not run
that comparison** — the accuracy numbers that belong in a table here need API
keys I do not have. `make eval` with `--pairs` is where they would go.

## Honest notes

**Five languages and three processes is more than this strictly needs.** Each
piece is here for a reason that can be stated in a sentence, but there is a
version of "why is this in C" where the true answer is "because I wanted to
write C". The real answer: pixel-level geometric transforms are a legitimate C
use case, and implementing perspective rectification, Sauvola binarisation and a
Hough transform is the point — calling `cv2.warpPerspective` would have taken an
afternoon and taught nothing.

**Two bugs the tests missed and running it caught.** The deskew estimator swept
angles at 1° while the profile peak is about 1° wide, so a real 2.5° skew fell
between samples and was reported as no skew at all. And perspective
rectification leaves a hard seam at the page border — a perfectly horizontal
line hundreds of pixels long — which produced a large spurious peak at exactly
0°, sitting right where a page needing no correction would peak. Both are fixed,
and both now have tests. Similar story in the browser: uploading a page opened
it while still queued, so the editor loaded empty text and never refreshed.

**What is not here.** No authentication — this binds to loopback and assumes a
TLS terminator in front. No arrows or marginalia; that is genuinely hard, the
variance is brutal, and it can absorb a week to produce something that works on
four pages and fails on the fifth. Strikethrough alone was the contained piece
worth taking. PDF rasterising shells out to `pdftoppm` or `mutool` rather than
embedding a renderer.

## Layout

```
preprocess/   C: perspective, deskew, binarisation, rule detection, + tests
rescorer/     Python: alignment, confusion matrix, lexicon, LM, decoder, eval
service/      Go: API, worker pool, SQLite, providers, layout, SSE
web/          TypeScript: CodeMirror editor with the confidence underlines
scripts/      the end-to-end demo
deploy/       Docker Compose and systemd units
```
